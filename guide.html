How to play

One orb, thrown and recalled. Everything below follows from that.

The run

Throw it, then get it back

You have one orb and it is both the weapon and the problem. Thrown, it flies until something stops it, bouncing off walls and enemies and losing speed the whole time. Held, you have a melee swing and nothing else. Nearly every card in the game is an answer to some version of the question "what happens between the throw and the catch".

Recall is a second weapon

Holding right click hauls the orb back through everything in the way, and a returning orb hits just as hard as a thrown one. Releasing early keeps the speed it gathered, so a recall is also how you re-aim without picking the orb up. Walking into a moving orb catches it — you do not have to wait for it to come to rest.

A wave, then two cards

Clear the field and the upgrade screen opens with two choices, a third of the time a third, and on every third pick a fourth card that is a utility ability rather than a passive. Rerolls and banishes accumulate on a timer rather than being spent currency, so a bad screen late in a run is a smaller disaster than a bad screen early.

It gets specific, not just bigger

Waves ramp, but the interesting pressure comes from named events: a modifier that changes the rules of one wave, a designed wave built from one kind of enemy, and a fixed boss every tenth. None of them are simply "the same wave with more health".

The upgrade screen

Clearing a wave opens it. You are offered two cards, and on every 3rd screen a further card that is a utility ability rather than a passive — you carry one of those at a time, so taking a second replaces the first.

Rerolls redraw the screen; banishes remove a card from the pool for the rest of the run. Both accumulate on a timer rather than being spent currency: you gain a reroll every 2 waves and a banish every 4, capped at 9 and 9. A bad screen late in a run is therefore a much smaller disaster than a bad screen early, which is the intent — the early screens are where a build is decided.

Rarity is a draw weight, not a power ranking. A card is offered in proportion to it: common 40 · uncommon 28 · rare 16 · epic 8 · legendary 3. Cards at their stack cap, cards whose prerequisite you do not own, and cards that conflict with something you already took are all removed from the draw rather than offered and wasted.

Staying alive

Bodies drop hearts, and the chance depends on how badly you need one — it reads your health fraction, so the field stays clean while you are healthy and starts paying out when you are not.

Elite kill×5 capped at 85%A fight you chose to take should pay for itself.
Boss kill4 drops guaranteedOne heart for two minutes of work would read as an insult.

A heart lives 13s, flashes for the last 3.5s, drifts to you inside 130px at 520px/s and is collected at 34px. At most 8 sit on the field at once, so a wipe in a big wave cannot carpet the ground.

The drop rate reads your health fraction, so it is a rubber band rather than a constant. Shield drops only roll for a build that can actually hold them — without Overflow or Kinetic Plating there is nowhere to put the points, and a pickup that does nothing is worse than no pickup at all.

Healing goes somewhere. Player:heal banks the excess so Overflow can turn it into shield instead of letting it evaporate at the cap. Shield absorbs before health, and draws as plates over the bar. Mitigation — Stoneskin, Last Stand — runs through a single mitigate() funnel, so two sources cannot silently multiply into immunity.
